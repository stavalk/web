Heat treatment is where a bench vise's durability is decided. The cast iron body is strong as-cast, but the jaws, screw, and slidable parts need deliberate metallurgy to perform through years of workshop abuse. This article explains the heat treatment operations in Stavalk's production line — why they exist, what they achieve, and how the numbers are verified.

## What 58–62 HRC actually means

Hardness is measured on the Rockwell C scale, and 58–62 HRC is the working range for hardened bench vise jaws. Below this range, jaws deform or dent under clamping pressure on hardened materials; above it, they become brittle and can crack under the hammer blows and prying that workshop vises routinely absorb. The 58–62 window is the metallurgical sweet spot: hard enough to indent steel, tough enough not to fracture.

## Induction hardening of the jaws

Stavalk jaws are machined from 45# forged steel — a medium-carbon steel with roughly 0.45% carbon, chosen precisely because it responds well to hardening heat treatments. Hardening is done by induction: the jaw is heated rapidly by an electromagnetic coil to austenitizing temperature, then quenched. The rapid heating confines the treatment to the gripping surface and the immediately adjacent metal, so the jaw keeps a strong, fatigue-resistant heart while the working face reaches the target hardness.

## Draw tempering after quenching

As-quenched steel is extremely hard but also brittle, which is exactly what a jaw should not be. Immediately after quenching, each jaw is draw-tempered at a controlled temperature. Tempering slightly softens the martensitic structure, trading a small amount of hardness at the top of the range for a large gain in toughness. This is the step that keeps the jaw hard on the face yet resistant to the impact and prying that real workshops deliver.

## Annealing and stress relief for castings and steel

Not every part is hardened. Cast iron bodies and machined slides benefit from stress-relief annealing: the part is held at a moderate temperature and slowly cooled, releasing internal stresses locked in during casting and machining. The result is a dimensionally stable body that will not distort in service, which matters for keeping the slide channel true and the mounting base flat over years of use.

## Verification and control

Heat treatment is only as good as its verification. Every jaw lot is hardness-tested on a Rockwell tester, with samples taken across the working face to confirm the 58–62 HRC range is met across the whole surface. Furnace and induction parameters are recorded per batch and traceable to material certificates, and parts found outside the range are rejected rather than blended into the stream. Where a product spec requires it, hardness certificates accompany the shipment.

## How heat treatment protects your product

For an OEM buyer, the practical takeaway is simple. A vise's jaws are the surface that actually clamps your customers' work, and the heat treatment is what makes them durable instead of decorative. When you evaluate a bench vise, ask the factory for its hardness test report — the number tells you whether the jaws will hold up through the warranty period and beyond. At Stavalk, that report is standard documentation on every shipment.
